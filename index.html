The complete training guide

How to stop subvocalizing while reading

You're reading these words right now, and there's a good chance you're "hearing" them in your head. That silent inner voice is called subvocalization — and it's one of the biggest bottlenecks keeping you from reading faster.

The average person reads at 200–250 words per minute, roughly the speed of normal speech. When you subvocalize, you're limited to how fast you can speak internally. But your brain can process visual information much faster — if you learn to bypass the inner voice.

The goal isn't to erase the inner voice. Some inner speech helps comprehension, especially for complex material. The aim is to reduce it enough that it stops being a bottleneck — reading at 400–600 WPM while still understanding what you read.

Why subvocalization happens

It isn't a bad habit you picked up — it's how you learned to read. As children we read aloud, then whispered, then moved our lips, and finally reached pure mental speech. For most people, development stopped there.

The inner voice connected written symbols to sounds you already knew. But after years of reading, you no longer need that step for familiar words. Skilled readers subvocalize selectively — only on difficult words or key ideas — while processing common words by sight.

The most effective method: RSVP

Rapid Serial Visual Presentation flashes words one at a time, faster than you can pronounce them internally. This forces your brain to process words visually rather than phonetically. It's the most researched technique for reducing subvocalization.

A simple weekly plan

Week 1–2: Start at 300 WPM, just above your natural pace. Practice 5–10 minutes daily. Get comfortable with words arriving faster than usual.

Week 3–4: Push to 400–450 WPM. Subvocalization becomes nearly impossible. You'll feel like you're missing words — that's the point. Keep sessions short.

Week 5+: Alternate sprints at 500+ WPM with comfortable runs at 350–400. Test your real-world speed every two weeks to track transfer.

Techniques that help

Occupy your inner voice. Humming, silently counting, or chewing gum ties up the speech areas of your brain. Use a pacer. Move a finger under the text faster than you'd read. Widen your span with the Peripheral and Schulte drills. Start easy — train on light material before dense text.

Common mistakes

Going too fast too soon, expecting instant results, and sacrificing all comprehension. Increase pace in 25–50 WPM steps only when the current speed feels comfortable, and never read so fast you retain nothing. Aim for the fastest pace where you still understand 70–80% of the material.

Realistic results: after 2–4 weeks of daily practice, many people raise reading speed by 50–100% with similar comprehension. Transfer from RSVP to normal reading is typically 60–70% — process 600 WPM in RSVP and you might read normal text around 400 WPM. Consistency beats intensity.
